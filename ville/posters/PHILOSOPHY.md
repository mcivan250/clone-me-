# Field Notation

*A design philosophy for objects that document what refuses to be kept.*

---

There is a discipline that does not exist, and this is its paperwork. Field Notation
borrows the visual authority of the survey — the plate number, the instrument
reading, the contour interval, the specimen grid — and turns it on subjects that no
instrument has ever measured: an hour, a room, a feeling that arrived and left. The
movement holds that anything can be studied if it is attended to closely enough, and
that the act of notating an ephemeral thing with clinical seriousness is itself a form
of devotion. The result must never look like a joke about science. It must look like
the genuine article: a plate lifted from a monograph published by an institution that
was never founded, meticulously crafted by a hand that has done this a thousand times.

**Space and form.** The page is a laboratory bench, not a canvas. A fixed margin runs
all the way around and is never broken — the discipline of the frame is the first
evidence of expertise. Within it, one dominant field holds the observation, and the
rest of the page is administration: a header that identifies the plate, a footer that
records the conditions. The field itself is built from patient accumulation — hundreds
of marks, rings, ticks, or cells, each one placed by rule rather than by whim, so that
structure emerges from repetition the way a pattern emerges from data. Nothing is
scattered. Everything is counted. The viewer should sense, without being told, that
someone sat with this for a very long time.

**Color and material.** A severe, finite ink set, declared in advance and never
exceeded — the constraint of the press, not the freedom of the screen. Each plate
elects one ink to lead and demotes the others to annotation, so a series reads as
variations struck from the same limited palette rather than a set of different
pictures. Colour carries meaning or it does not appear: a hue is a channel, a
temperature, a warning, a redaction. Ground is deep and matte; ink sits on it the way
pigment sits on uncoated stock, with the faint break-up of a screen that has been
printed slightly too hard. This is the labour of a master printer, not a filter.

**Scale and rhythm.** Extreme contrast of size is the movement's signature: one
utterance at monumental scale, everything else whispered at the threshold of
legibility. The large form is the subject; the small type is the apparatus that proves
someone was measuring. Between them, nothing — the middle register is deliberately
empty, because a page with three competing sizes has no hierarchy at all. The small
type must reward the reader who leans in, carrying real particulars: a time, a count,
a condition, a limit. Invented filler is the one unforgivable failure. Every number on
the page must be true.

**Composition and balance.** Asymmetry held by an invisible grid. The observation field
is placed off-centre and allowed to bleed toward one edge, while the opposite edge is
kept generous and quiet, so the page leans without falling. Alignment is absolute:
every baseline, tick and rule answers to the same underlying measure, and a single
element permitted to sit slightly outside that measure becomes the composition's one
deliberate tension. Nothing overlaps. Nothing crowds. Nothing touches the margin. These
are not preferences — they are the difference between a designed object and an
arranged one, and the eye registers the difference instantly even when it cannot name
it.

**Visual hierarchy.** The order of reading is fixed and must never be ambiguous: the
field first, caught from across a room; then the one large word; then, on approach, the
apparatus. Text earns its place only by being necessary — a name, a date, a rule, a
count — and is set as a visual element with the same care as any drawn mark, never as
a paragraph asking to be read. The finished plate should survive two distances: it must
work at ten metres as pure form, and reward a reader at thirty centimetres with
detail they did not expect. Achieving both in one object is the whole craft, and it
is the product of painstaking, unglamorous, master-level attention — the kind that
shows only in the absence of anything wrong.
